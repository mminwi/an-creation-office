SOP: A pneumatic assembly fixture with air-logic controls. Two-hand start, guard locking, cylinders, the fixture itself, machining, and prove-out. Revision 1.
Index: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop.txt
Lines are marked "office", "published" (with the source to check) or "Mike".

WHAT THIS IS (office)
An operator loads parts into nests, closes a hinged door, presses two buttons, and air cylinders press the parts together. No electronics: air logic (pneumatic valves that do AND, OR, NOT, timing and memory) runs the sequence. Simple to build, but the safety rules for presses and pinch points apply in full. The general machine-project path still holds (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-automation-project.txt): specification, proof of concept on real parts, build, prove-out, buy-off.

THE SEQUENCE IN WORDS FIRST (office; Mike)
Write the sequence of operation before choosing valves: door open and parts loaded; door closed and locked; both buttons pressed together; cylinders extend; hold (if a dwell is needed); cylinders retract; door unlocks; operator unloads. For each step: what must be true to go on, and what happens if air is lost, a button is released, or the door is forced. Draw it as a pneumatic schematic (ISO 1219 symbols) with every valve, sensor valve and line.

TWO-HAND CONTROL (published: ISO 13851 two-hand control devices, types I to III; ANSI B11.19 for safeguarding; the risk assessment decides the type)
- Both buttons must be pressed within a short time of each other (concurrent actuation), each must be released before the next cycle (no tie-down), and releasing either one stops or reverses the motion if the hazard is still present.
- Place the buttons far enough apart (and guarded) that one hand or an elbow and hand can't press both, and far enough from the hazard that the hands can't reach it before motion stops (safety distance).
- Pneumatic two-hand control modules exist; check which type they meet against what the risk assessment requires.
- With a locked door that keeps hands out during the stroke, the two-hand control also keeps the operator's hands busy; the risk assessment decides whether either is enough alone.

THE DOOR: INTERLOCKING AND GUARD LOCKING (published: ISO 14119 interlocking devices with guard locking; ISO 13849-1 for the control's performance level)
- The door must be closed before the cycle can start, and locked while anything can move. It must not unlock until motion has stopped and the cylinders are back (or pressure is released).
- Use an interlock rated for the job (pneumatic guard-locking interlocks and safety valves exist); a plain limit valve that can be defeated with a screwdriver is not enough.
- Check what happens if air is lost mid-cycle: does the door stay locked with the cylinder extended and pressurized? Can the operator be trapped? Plan the release.

AIR SUPPLY AND ENERGY ISOLATION (published: ISO 4414 pneumatic safety; OSHA 29 CFR 1910.147 lockout)
- At the inlet: a lockable shut-off with exhaust (dump valve) for lockout, filter, regulator with gauge, and a soft-start valve so cylinders don't slam when air is turned on.
- Separate regulators where the pressing force must be controlled differently from the clamps or the door lock; lock the regulator setting that controls force.
- Stored energy: cylinders can hold pressure after shut-off; trapped air must be released (or the risk accepted and labeled) before anyone works on the fixture.
- Valves with spring return so the fixture goes to a known, safe state on air loss.

CYLINDERS AND FORCE (office)
- Measure the force needed to press the parts together on real parts (a simple force gauge or a load cell on a test stand), at the worst case of the part tolerances. Then size each cylinder: force = pressure x piston area (minus losses and the rod side on retract), with margin, at the pressure the plant reliably supplies.
- Five parts pressed at once: one cylinder with a pressing plate, or five cylinders? One cylinder needs the plate stiff and parallel so all five see the same force and depth; five cylinders give independent force but cost more.
- Speed: flow controls (usually metering the exhaust) set a smooth approach; cushions or shock absorbers at the ends of stroke.
- Confirm the press is complete: a fixed stop sets depth, or end-of-stroke sensing (limit valves) confirms it before the door unlocks; a pressure switch or gauge confirms force.

THE FIXTURE ITSELF (office; Mike)
- Locate each part the same way every time: a solid datum scheme (the 3-2-1 principle), nests shaped to the part so it only goes in one way (error-proofing), and clearance for loading by hand.
- Five stations: align the five nests to the five pressing tools; tolerance the plate and the tools so every station gets the same depth. Run the stack-up (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-tolerance-stack.txt).
- Materials: aluminum plate (6061) for the base and structure; plastic (acetal, UHMW) or hardened inserts in the nests where parts rub or wear, and plastic where metal would mark the part.
- The hinged lid or door: a sturdy hinge, a hold-open (gas spring or detent) so it can't drop on hands, and a latch that the lock acts on.
- Commercial parts first (locating pins, bushings, hinges, gas springs from catalog makers such as MISUMI); machine only what must be custom.
- Ergonomics: loading height and reach, button height, the operator's view of the nests, the force and repetition of loading.

MACHINING AND DRAWINGS (office)
- Drawings with the datums and tolerances that matter (nest locations and depth, pressing tool alignment), looser everywhere else to keep cost down; general tolerances in the title block.
- Ask the shop about material, finish (clear or hard anodize on aluminum wear surfaces), edge breaks and deburring, and inspection of the critical dimensions.
- Typical machined parts: base plate, nest blocks, pressing plate, cylinder mounts, door frame. Order long-lead parts (cylinders, safety valves, the two-hand module) early.

PROVE IT OUT (office)
- Dry cycle without parts: every step of the sequence, button timing, the door lock, air loss at each step, an e-stop or dump.
- With parts: press quality at all five stations (depth or a pull-out check), no damage to parts, cycle time.
- Safety functions tested and recorded (two-hand timing and release, door lock, air loss) before buy-off.
- Hand-over: the pneumatic schematic, drawings, regulator settings, spare parts list, the risk assessment and an operator instruction with pictures.

QUESTIONS TO ASK (office)
What force does each part need, and what is the plant air pressure at its worst? One cylinder or five? How is a complete press confirmed? What two-hand type and door locking does the risk assessment require? What happens on air loss with the cylinder down? How does the operator load all the parts (five of each) quickly and correctly every time?
