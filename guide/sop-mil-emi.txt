SOP: Military EMI/EMC (MIL-STD-461) for a small soldier-worn power device. Revision 1 — October 13, 2026.
Index: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop.txt
Lines are marked "office" or "published" (with the source to check).

THE STANDARD (published: MIL-STD-461H, April 2026, superseded 461G; many contracts still call out 461G or older. The contract edition governs.)
MIL-STD-461 sets the interface limits for a single piece of equipment: what it may emit and what it must withstand. MIL-STD-464 covers the whole system (platform E3, bonding, lightning); a subsystem usually meets 461 and supports 464 where the customer asks.
Requirement names: CE = conducted emissions, CS = conducted susceptibility, RE = radiated emissions, RS = radiated susceptibility.

REQUIREMENTS OFTEN INVOKED FOR SOLDIER-WORN GROUND EQUIPMENT (published; the applicability table in the standard and the contract decide)
- CE102: conducted emissions on power leads (the noise the device puts back onto the battery cable).
- CS101: conducted susceptibility on power leads, low frequency ripple.
- CS114: bulk cable injection (RF currents on cables).
- CS115: bulk cable injection, impulse.
- CS116: damped sinusoidal transients on cables and power leads.
- CS118: personnel-borne electrostatic discharge (added in 461G; equipment a person handles).
- RE102: radiated emissions, electric field.
- RS103: radiated susceptibility, electric field.
- Ask whether RE101/RS101 (magnetic fields), CS106 (power-lead transients) or others are invoked for this application.

WHERE THIS KIND OF DEVICE USUALLY FAILS (office, general practice)
- Switching power conversion: the DC-DC converter's switching edges and harmonics show up in CE102 and RE102. Layout, input filtering and snubbing matter more than the enclosure for conducted emissions.
- Cables: at the frequencies in RE102 and RS103, the cables are usually the antennas, not the box. Shielded cable with the shield terminated 360 degrees at the connector backshell, and filtering where each cable enters, are the main tools.
- Seams and openings in the enclosure: a metal box only shields if its seams are continuous at the frequencies of concern. Gaps between fasteners, painted or anodized mating surfaces, and connector cutouts leak. (sop-mil-enclosure.txt covers gaskets and finishes.)
- Grounding and bonding: the shield, the enclosure and the circuit reference need a deliberate plan, decided with the electrical engineer; a shield grounded only through a pigtail wire performs poorly at high frequency.
- ESD (CS118): exposed metal, connector shells and seams a hand can touch; transient protection at the connectors.
- Data lines (USB and similar): fast edges radiate; common-mode chokes, proper shield termination and controlled routing help.

THE PATH TO PASSING (office)
1. Get the exact requirements and limits from the contract (edition, which requirements, limit curves, any tailoring).
2. Design review against the checklist above with the electrical engineer, early, before the enclosure and cables are frozen.
3. Pre-compliance scans on early units (near-field probes, a LISN for conducted emissions, a current probe on cables) to find problems while they are cheap to fix.
4. Formal test at an accredited EMI lab, with an approved EMI test procedure; the customer may require the procedure (EMITP) and report (EMITR) as data items.
5. If it fails, find the source (conducted or radiated, which cable, which frequency), fix one thing at a time, and record each change against the configuration (sop-mil-program.txt).

WHAT THE OFFICE KEEPS (office)
The requirement list and status, the pre-scan and test dates, which units and configurations were tested, pass/fail by requirement and the report number. The detailed test data and design specifics may be controlled; keep them in the company's system (sop-mil-program.txt, EXPORT CONTROL FIRST).

HANDS OFF TO (office)
The electrical engineer for circuit, layout and filter design; the EMI test lab for procedures and testing; sop-mil-enclosure.txt for the mechanical shielding.
