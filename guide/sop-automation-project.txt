THE PATH OF A MACHINE PROJECT (office)
1. Requirements: a user requirement specification (URS) or machine specification: what parts, what rate (parts per minute, cycle time), what it must check and reject, accuracy, changeover, utilities (power, air), floor space, the plant's standards (PLC brand, HMI, safety, networking, guarding color, labeling), and acceptance criteria. Every later argument is settled by this document; get it signed.
2. Concept and risk: layout, the sequence of operation, a first machine risk assessment (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-machine-safety.txt), and the hard problems listed early (part feeding, vision, dispensing are usually the hardest).
3. Design reviews: mechanical, controls and software, with the customer at agreed points; record decisions and open items.
4. Long-lead items: servo motors and drives, cameras and lighting, the PLC and safety parts, the enclosure, dispensing equipment, custom machined parts. Order early; track promised dates.
5. Build and debug: mechanical assembly, panel build, wiring, I/O check, motion tuning, then process development with real parts (feeding, vision, dispensing are tuned with production parts, not drawings).
6. Factory acceptance test (FAT) at the builder: run the agreed test (rate, reject accuracy, run-off quantity, safety functions, alarms and recovery) with the customer present. Record results and a punch list.
7. Ship, install, and site acceptance test (SAT): repeat the key tests in the plant with the plant's utilities, air and network.
8. Hand-over: documentation package (electrical and pneumatic schematics, mechanical drawings, bill of materials, spare parts list, PLC and HMI programs with a backup, manuals, the risk assessment and safety validation), training, and warranty start.

BUILD, PROTOTYPE AND PROVE IT OUT (office; Mike)
Prove the risky parts first
- Before the full machine is designed, run proof-of-concept tests on the hardest stations with real production parts: feeding through the tube, holding the part in a nest, the vision inspection under the chosen lighting, and the adhesive dot (dispense and cure). A bench rig, borrowed equipment or the supplier's lab is enough. Record what worked, the settings, and what is still uncertain.
- Freeze the concept only when each risky station has worked on real parts; the rest is engineering.
Build
- Mechanical: assemble to drawings, check critical alignments (nest to camera, nest to dispenser, wheel runout and indexing accuracy), and record adjustments.
- Panel: build to the schematics, then a point-to-point wiring check before power, insulation and continuity checks, and torque on terminals.
First power-up (safely, in steps)
- With motors and outputs disconnected: verify incoming power, power supply voltages, the PLC powering up and the safety circuit.
- I/O checkout: every input and output, one at a time, against the I/O list, with the device it belongs to.
- Motion commissioning: drive set-up, direction, limits, homing, then tuning with the real load at low speed first.
- Safety functions: test each one (e-stop, interlocks, light curtains, safe stopping) and record the validation before anyone runs the machine in automatic.
Debug and process development
- Dry cycle without parts until the sequence, timing and fault recovery are solid.
- Then run with parts: tune feeding, nest hold, vision lighting and limits, dispense volume and cure. Change one thing at a time and record each setting.
- Prove recovery: jams, a missing part, an empty adhesive reservoir, a camera fault, an e-stop mid-cycle, power loss. Each must end safe, with no bad part passed as good.
Prove it runs
- Rate: hold the agreed rate over a long run, not a burst.
- Endurance: a continuous run (the hours or part count agreed in the specification), tracking every stop by cause and the mean time between assists (how often an operator has to step in).
- Quality: the vision challenge set (false rejects and false accepts), dot size and position data with capability (Cpk) where there is a numeric limit, and a sample of good parts checked independently.
- Fix what the run shows, then repeat the affected tests; the punch list is closed before the FAT, not during it.

IS THE PRODUCT REGULATED? (office; ask at the start)
- If the product the machine makes is a medical device (many consumer health products are), the customer's quality system may require equipment and process validation: IQ (installed correctly), OQ (operates across its range), PQ (performs consistently on production) and validation of the software that makes accept/reject decisions. (published: ISO 13485 clauses on process validation and software validation; the FDA's quality system regulation, now aligned with ISO 13485. The customer's procedures govern.)
- A vision system that decides good from bad is exactly the kind of process the customer may need validated: plan the challenge parts and the evidence early.
- If not regulated, the FAT and SAT with agreed acceptance criteria still apply.

WHAT THE OFFICE KEEPS (office)
The specification status and open questions, the sequence of operation in words, the risk register, long-lead tracking, design review notes, FAT/SAT protocols and results (numbers and pass/fail), the punch list, and the hand-over checklist.

QUESTIONS TO ASK EARLY (office)
- Rate and uptime: what parts per minute, how many shifts, what downtime is acceptable, what happens upstream and downstream when this machine stops?
- Rejects: what is a bad part, who defines it with sample parts, what false-reject and false-accept rates are acceptable, and how is a reject proven to have gone to the reject line?
- Changeover: one part or several sizes? How fast must a changeover be?
- The plant's standards: PLC and HMI brand, network, safety, labeling, spare-part commonality, guarding and colors.
- Utilities: voltage and phase, compressed air pressure and quality, network connection, floor loading, clean-room or washdown needs.
- Who owns the process recipe (glue amount, vision limits) and who may change it?
