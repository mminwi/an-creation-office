SOP: Ruggedizing a commercial electronic product for military use. An off-the-shelf enclosure and connectors, sealing, environmental testing, and keeping the product's own approvals. Revision 1 — October 13, 2026.
Index: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop.txt
Lines are marked "office" or "published" (with the source to check).

WHAT THIS IS (office)
An existing commercial product's circuit board moves from its own commercial housing into an off-the-shelf rugged enclosure with off-the-shelf sealed connectors, so it survives military environments: water, washdown, desert heat and sun, cold, sand and dust, shock and vibration. The electronics are someone else's design; this project owns the box, the seals, the connectors, the mounting, and the proof that it all survives (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-mil-environmental.txt).

PIN DOWN THE REQUIREMENT, INCLUDING WHAT ISN'T WRITTEN (office)
- Get the environments in writing: which MIL-STD-810 methods and levels, or an IP rating, or both. "Doesn't need to be watertight" usually changes once washdown, rain, immersion and storage are discussed; ask now, in writing, because the enclosure choice depends on it.
- An IP rating and a MIL-STD-810 test are different things. IP67 (IEC 60529) is temporary immersion, commonly 1 m for 30 minutes at room temperature; MIL-STD-810 Method 512 immersion can use a temperature difference (the item hotter than the water, so cooling pulls water in) and the depth the contract sets. Washdown is a jet test (IPX5/IPX6, or IPX9K for high-pressure hot water under ISO 20653). Be clear which one the customer means.
- Ask about export control and the customer's quality requirements early (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-mil-program.txt): a military version of a commercial product may change its export classification.

THE PRODUCT'S OWN APPROVALS (published: IEC 61010-1 safety of electrical measuring equipment, with IEC 61010-2-030 for measuring circuits and measurement categories CAT II, III, IV; FCC/CE EMC; confirm what the product holds today)
- A power logger measures mains voltage. Its safety approval depends on the housing as well as the board: creepage and clearance distances, insulation, accessible parts, and the connectors carrying measured voltage (their voltage and measurement-category rating). A new enclosure and new connectors can void that approval.
- Ask: what approvals does the commercial product hold (safety listing, measurement category, EMC)? Does the military version need them, or something different? Who re-evaluates them after the change (the board's maker, a test lab)?
- EMC: a metal box may shield better than the old housing; a plastic one may not. If the customer requires EMC or MIL-STD-461 testing, plan it (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-mil-emi.txt).

KNOW THE BOARD'S OWN LIMITS FIRST (office)
Read the commercial product's published specifications and manual, and record them in the project repository. For a portable power logger they typically show:
- Operating temperature: a commercial range (for example, a little below freezing to about 60 C) is often narrower than military cold and desert heat. Then the enclosure must protect the board: a heater or insulation for cold, shade, light color and conduction for heat, or a conversation with the customer about the range.
- Humidity "non-condensing": a sealed box must keep condensation off the board (dry assembly, desiccant, coating).
- Self-powered from the measured line: many loggers take their power from the voltage they measure (for example, up to 600 V, measurement category III). Then the mains-voltage connectors on the new box carry hazardous voltage and must be rated for it (voltage, measurement category, creepage and clearance, touch-safe when unmated).
- Low-level sensor inputs: current sensors (CTs, Rogowski coils) and analog inputs carry millivolt or milliamp signals; keep their wiring away from the mains wiring inside the box and use connectors that keep the sensor's shielding and keying.
- Communications: USB and Ethernet need sealed connectors (rated sealed USB and RJ45 versions exist); Wi-Fi won't get out of a closed metal box, so it needs an external antenna and a sealed antenna connector, or a non-metal window.
- Housing material and flammability: the original plastic housing may carry a flammability rating (such as UL 94 V-0) that the safety approval relies on; the new enclosure must meet the same or better.

CHOOSING THE OFF-THE-SHELF ENCLOSURE (office; published: NEMA 250 enclosure types, IEC 60529 IP code)
- Material: die-cast aluminum (strong, shields, conducts heat, needs a finish for salt fog), polycarbonate or fiberglass (light, corrosion-proof, doesn't shield, check UV resistance and low-temperature impact), or stainless steel.
- Ratings: IP66/IP67/IP68 and NEMA 4X/6P; a rating applies to the box as sold, before you drill holes in it. Every hole you add must be sealed to the same level.
- Size: room for the board, connectors' rear depth, cable bends, mounting, and air for heat; plus space for a desiccant or vent if needed.
- Mounting: how it fixes to the vehicle, rack or stand; mounting tabs, feet or a bracket that survives vibration.

OFF-THE-SHELF CONNECTORS (published: MIL-DTL-38999 and MIL-DTL-26482 military circular connectors; commercial IP68 circulars such as M12; check current revisions and makers' data)
- Choose by the signals and power carried (including measured mains voltage and current sensor inputs: voltage and current rating, measurement category), mating cycles, mis-mating prevention (keying), and sealing.
- Sealing has two cases: mated (plug in) and unmated (plug out). Most connectors are only sealed when mated; unmated, they need sealing caps, ideally on a lanyard so they don't get lost. Ask whether the unit must survive immersion with cables disconnected and caps on.
- Panel sealing: an O-ring or flat gasket under the receptacle flange or jam nut, the hole size and flatness per the connector maker, and the right torque.
- Cable entries without a connector: rated cable glands sized to the cable jacket.
- Wiring inside: from the connectors to the board's existing connectors or terminals, with strain relief; avoid modifying the board when possible.

PRESSURE, CONDENSATION AND HEAT (office)
- A sealed box breathes with temperature and altitude. Heated in the sun then hit by cold rain or immersed, it can pull water past the seals. A pressure-equalizing vent with a waterproof membrane (expanded PTFE) lets air through but not water; it is a hole, so choose one rated to the immersion depth.
- Condensation inside a sealed box: dry assembly, a desiccant pack, conformal coating on the board (ask the board's maker), and drain paths if allowed.
- Heat: a sealed box in desert sun gets hot (MIL-STD-810 Methods 501 and 505); check the board's rated temperature against the inside temperature, not the outside one. A light color, a sunshield, or a metal box that conducts heat out helps.
- Cold: battery (if any), display and connector seals at the low temperature.

DISPLAYS, BUTTONS AND BATTERIES (office)
- Any display or button on the old housing needs a sealed equivalent: a window with a gasket, a sealed membrane switch, or sealed pushbuttons rated to the same level, or remote it.
- If the logger has an internal battery: access for charging or replacement without breaking the seal, and lithium battery shipping rules (UN 38.3).

MOUNTING THE BOARD (office; https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-mil-electronics-build.txt)
Standoffs or a mounting plate inside the box, secured for shock and vibration, with heavy parts staked where needed, and the board's existing mounting holes used if possible.

PROVING IT (office)
- Early: leak test the modified enclosure with the connectors installed (immersion or a pressure-decay test) before the formal tests.
- Formal: the MIL-STD-810 methods the requirement names, at an accredited lab, plus functional checks before and after each (the logger still measures accurately). Accuracy checks belong to the board's maker's procedure.
- Production: a leak test on every unit (pressure decay is quick and objective) and torque records on connectors and lid.

QUESTIONS TO ASK (office)
Which environments and levels, in writing, including washdown and immersion depth and temperature? Sealed with cables disconnected? What approvals does the commercial product hold, and do they carry over? Metal or plastic box, and does EMC matter? Where will it be mounted, and how hot will it get inside in the sun? Does it need a vent? Any display, buttons or battery to deal with? Who owns the board, its firmware and its accuracy?
