SOP: A charger for power-tool lithium-ion battery packs, with USB-C power. How the circuit is set up, the decisions, the protections, and testing it safely. Revision 1.
Index: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop.txt
Lines are marked "office" or "published" (with the source to check).

HOW TO USE THIS (office)
Walk the owner through the circuit block by block so they understand each part and why it's there. Lithium-ion packs store a lot of energy; a charger that overcharges, charges a hot or frozen pack, or keeps pushing current into a fault can start a fire. The design copies tested reference designs, adds an independent second protection, and is tested in steps before it ever charges a pack unattended. General board steps: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-board-design.txt

KNOW THE PACK FIRST (office; record it in the repository)
- Cells in series: an "18 V" or "20 V max" tool pack is usually five lithium-ion cells in series (5S): about 18 V nominal and about 21 V full at 4.2 V per cell. Some packs have parallel strings (5S2P, 5S3P) for more capacity. Confirm from the pack's label and by measuring.
- The terminals: besides plus and minus, tool packs usually have a temperature sensor (an NTC thermistor) and often an identification resistor and cell-tap (balance) contacts. Makers don't publish pinouts. Measure them on the owner's pack (resistance from each terminal to minus with a meter, pack out of any tool) and write them down; don't trust a pinout found online without measuring.
- What protects the pack: some packs have electronics that cut off on a fault; many leave charge control to the charger. Assume the pack does not protect itself while charging, until you know otherwise.
- Charge limits: the pack's rated charge current (the maker's own charger's output is a good guide), its capacity in amp-hours, and the cells' charge temperature range (commonly about 0 to 45 C).
- The mechanical connection: use a purchased mating adapter or dock for that battery platform, mounted in the enclosure. Don't 3D-print the electrical contacts.

THE BLOCKS (office; published: the chip makers' data sheets and evaluation boards)
1. Power in. Two choices, or both:
   - A listed external DC power supply (for example 24 V), plugged into a barrel jack or connector. The board never touches mains voltage.
   - USB-C with USB Power Delivery. A USB-C port gives only 5 V unless a USB PD controller on the board asks the charger for more: usually 20 V (up to 3 A with a normal cable, up to 5 A with a 5 A-rated cable, so 60 to 100 W). Standalone PD sink controllers (for example ST's STUSB4500, or TI's TPS25750 configured as a sink) negotiate this without firmware.
   - With both inputs, a power selector (ideal-diode OR or a power multiplexer chip) picks one and keeps them from feeding each other.
2. Input protection: a fuse, reverse-polarity protection on the DC jack, a surge clamp (TVS diode), and ESD protection on the USB-C connector.
3. The charger chip. Because a full 5S pack (about 21 V) is above USB-C's 20 V, the charger must step the voltage up as well as down: a buck-boost charger controller that handles the cell count (for example TI's BQ25756, rated for 1 to 14 cells, which can also run in reverse and supply USB-C power from the pack). With only a 24 V or higher adapter, a simpler step-down (buck) charger also works. The chip sets:
   - Charge voltage (per cell times cells; setting 4.1 V per cell instead of 4.2 gives margin and longer pack life for a little less capacity).
   - Charge current (at or below the pack's rating, and within what the input can supply).
   - Pre-charge for a deeply discharged pack, termination when the current tapers, and a safety timer.
   - Temperature: the pack's thermistor wired to the chip's temperature input, so charging stops when the pack is too cold or too hot (and reduces near the limits).
4. Independent second protection: the charger chip only sees the total pack voltage. A cell monitor chip (for example TI's BQ76920 family, 3 to 5 cells) reads each cell through the pack's cell-tap contacts and switches off charging if any cell goes over voltage, or if the cells are badly out of balance. This is the part a home-made charger most often lacks, and the one most worth adding. It works on its own, independent of the charger chip.
5. Pack detection and ID: charge only when a pack is present and its thermistor reads sensibly. Optionally read the ID resistor and refuse unknown packs.
6. Output switch and fuse on the pack side, so a fault can disconnect the pack.
7. Status: LEDs for power, charging, done and fault.
8. USB output (if the owner wants to charge a phone from the pack): either the charger chip's reverse mode feeding the USB-C port, or a separate step-down converter to 5 V with a USB-C source controller. Ask which the owner means by "USB": charging the pack from USB-C, charging a phone from the pack, or both.

THE DECISIONS TO MAKE WITH THE OWNER (office)
- Which inputs: USB-C PD, a DC power supply, or both?
- Charge current: faster charging means more heat in the board and the pack; a modest current (for example 2 to 3 A) keeps the first board simpler.
- USB output, and how much power?
- Microcontroller or not: a standalone charger configured with resistors is simplest; a small microcontroller adds a display or logging but also firmware to write and test.
- How the board and the pack get rid of heat in a 3D-printed box (vents, a metal plate, a lower current).

TESTING IT SAFELY, IN THIS ORDER (office)
1. Bare board: check for shorts between rails with a meter before any power.
2. Power from a bench supply with the current limit set low; check each rail.
3. Charger into a resistor or an electronic load instead of a pack: check charge voltage (it must stop at the set voltage), current, termination, timer and the temperature cut-off (swap the thermistor for resistors that simulate hot and cold).
4. The second protection: simulate a cell over voltage with a bench supply on the cell-tap input and confirm charging stops.
5. First real pack: an old or spare pack, on a non-combustible surface, outdoors or near an exit, with each cell's voltage and the pack's temperature measured during charging, and someone watching the whole time. Stop at anything unexpected.
6. Only after several full, watched charges with clean records, charge unattended, and still on a non-combustible surface.
Record every test in the job folder: settings, readings, result.

THE ENCLOSURE (office)
3D-printed enclosures are fine for a personal project, but common printing plastics burn. Keep the power parts away from the walls, give heat a way out, consider a flame-retardant filament, and never close the box tight over a hot board. The owner draws the enclosure around the board's STEP model (https://ancreationoffice.com/sop-ai-cad.txt).

RULES (published: 47 CFR 15.23 home-built devices; 47 CFR Part 15 for anything sold; IEC 62133-2, UL 2054 for battery packs)
A home-built device that isn't sold, built in quantities of five or fewer for personal use, doesn't need FCC equipment authorization, but it must not cause harmful interference. If it is ever sold, it needs FCC Part 15 compliance and, in practice, a safety listing; that is a different project.

NEVER (office)
- Never charge a pack without the thermistor connected and working.
- Never test a new charger on a pack unattended, indoors on a combustible surface, or on a damaged, swollen or hot pack.
- Never guess a terminal's function; measure it.
- Never design the mains (wall-voltage) side: use a listed power supply.
