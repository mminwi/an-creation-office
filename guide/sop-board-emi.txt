SOP: Clean power and low EMI on a small board with a switching converter. Layout rules, filtering, measuring ripple, and finding noise without a test lab. Revision 1.
Index: https://ancreationoffice.com/sop.txt
Lines are marked "office" or "published" (with the source to check).

WHY IT MATTERS (office)
A switching converter or charger turns current on and off hundreds of thousands of times a second. Done well, it is efficient and quiet. Done badly, it radiates radio noise, puts ripple on its output, upsets anything nearby, and can misbehave itself. Almost all of it is decided by the layout, and it costs nothing to get right on the first board, and a lot to fix afterwards.

THE LAYOUT RULES (published: the chip maker's layout guidelines; TI "Layout Guidelines for Switching Power Supplies"; Analog Devices layout notes; office for the summary)
- Find the loops where current switches (in a step-down converter: input capacitor, high-side switch, low-side switch, and back). Make that loop as small as physically possible, with the input capacitor right at the switches. This is the most important rule.
- One solid ground plane on the layer next to the power parts, unbroken under the converter. No slots or splits under switching parts or under signals that cross them.
- Keep the switch node (the copper between the switches and the inductor) small: it is the noisiest point on the board. Keep it away from the edges, connectors and sensitive traces.
- Feedback and sense traces: short, away from the inductor and switch node; current-sense lines run as a pair to the sense resistor (Kelvin connection).
- Use a shielded inductor.
- Copy the evaluation board's power-stage layout as closely as possible; it was tested.

FILTERING AND OPTIONS TO LEAVE ROOM FOR (office)
- Input filter at the input connector: a ferrite bead or small inductor with capacitors, so switching noise doesn't go out on the power cable (cables are the antennas).
- Footprints for an RC snubber across the switch node and a gate resistor, left empty or fitted later, to slow down the edges if the board is noisy.
- Spread-spectrum switching, if the chip offers it.
- ESD and surge protection at every connector a person touches (USB-C especially).
- Ceramic capacitors close to every chip's power pins.

MEASURING RIPPLE PROPERLY (office)
Measure output ripple with the oscilloscope's 20 MHz bandwidth limit on and the probe's long ground lead removed: touch the probe tip and its ground ring (or a short ground spring) right across the output capacitor. A long ground lead picks up noise that isn't there and makes a good supply look bad.

FINDING NOISE WITHOUT A LAB (office)
- The AM radio test: tune a portable AM radio between stations and bring it near the running board and its cables. Buzzing that changes with load points to the noisy part.
- A near-field probe set with a low-cost spectrum analyzer or software-defined radio shows where on the board the noise comes from. Compare before and after each change.
- A real test lab (pre-compliance scan) is only needed if the product will be sold.

RULES (published: 47 CFR 15.23 home-built devices; 47 CFR 15.5 no harmful interference; 47 CFR Part 15 Subpart B for products sold)
Built for personal use, five or fewer, not sold: no FCC authorization is needed, but the device must not cause harmful interference, and the builder should come as close to the Part 15 limits as sound engineering allows. A product for sale needs Part 15 testing.

QUESTIONS TO ASK (office)
Is the power stage laid out like the evaluation board? Is the hot loop as small as it can be? Is the ground plane solid under it? Is there an input filter at the connector? Are there snubber footprints? Has ripple been measured the right way? Does an AM radio buzz near it?
