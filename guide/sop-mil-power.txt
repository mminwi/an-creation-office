HOW TO USE THIS (office)
This is for asking the right questions, not designing the board. Work through the checklists with the owner: "Have we considered this? Have we looked at that?" Record each answer as answered, open (who owns it), or not applicable (why). Circuit design, schematics and analysis happen with the electrical engineer in the company's controlled environment; here keep only the question, its status and where the answer lives (sop-mil-program.txt, EXPORT CONTROL FIRST).

LITHIUM-ION CHARGING, THE BASICS (published: battery and charger IC makers' application notes, IEC 62133-2, UL 2054; office for the summary)
- Charge profile is constant current, then constant voltage (CC/CV). The charger delivers a set current until the cell reaches its charge voltage, then holds that voltage while the current tapers; charging ends when the current falls to a termination level or a timer expires.
- Charge voltage per cell is set by the cell chemistry and maker (commonly about 4.2 V per cell; some cells are rated lower or higher). Overcharging even slightly shortens life and raises safety risk. The cell maker's data sheet governs.
- Pre-charge: a deeply discharged cell is brought up gently at a low current before full current.
- Temperature: most lithium-ion cells must not be charged below about 0 °C or above roughly 45 °C, and many chargers reduce current or voltage near the ends of the range (a JEITA-style profile). A soldier-worn device sees cold. Cell data sheet governs.
- Protection inside the pack: over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current, short circuit and over-temperature, usually in the battery's own protection circuit and sometimes a second, independent level. Multi-cell packs balance cells.
- Smart batteries report state of charge, temperature, voltage, current and faults over SMBus (Smart Battery Data Specification); the charger can read the battery's requested charge voltage and current from it.

WHO DOES THE CHARGING IN THIS KIND OF DEVICE (office)
- Know exactly which block does what: the source battery (for example, a vest battery with its own protection and SMBus), this device (passes power through and provides a charging output), and the radio battery and whatever charger or "battery controller" sits in front of it. Each block has its own limits; the interface between them is where problems hide.
- Get the interface documents for each (input voltage range, maximum current, connector pinout, data protocol, required sequencing); don't infer them.

THE QUESTION CHECKLIST (office)
Source and power path
- What is the input voltage range from the source battery across its full state of charge, and at cold temperature? (A conformal wearable battery, for example, varies roughly 10 to 20 V by version and charge.)
- How much current can the source deliver continuously and at peak, and what does its protection do when exceeded?
- Pass-through: does the downstream device keep its full power while the charging output is loaded? What happens when both pull at once; who gets priority?
- What happens at hot-plug: inrush current, connector arcing, sequencing when cables are connected in any order?
- Reverse polarity, mis-mating and short circuits on each connector: what protects them?
- Transients on the cables (ESD, cable injection from MIL-STD-461 CS114/CS115/CS116): what clamps or filters them?
- Does the device drain the source when nothing is connected? What is its quiescent current?
Charging output
- What does the radio battery or its charger expect: voltage, current limit, communication, and charge termination? Is this device a power source only, or does it control the charge?
- Is the output current-limited and protected against a short, a damaged battery, or the wrong battery?
- Over-temperature: how hot do the device and the radio battery get while charging on a soldier in the sun? What derates?
- Cold: does charging stop or reduce below the cell's minimum charge temperature, and who measures that temperature?
- Efficiency: how much power is lost as heat in the conversion, and can the enclosure shed it?
Clean power and EMI
- What are the output ripple and noise limits, and are they specified at the radio's input?
- Is the DC-DC converter's switching frequency chosen with the EMI limits (CE102, RE102) in mind, and is there input and output filtering? (sop-mil-emi.txt)
- Does charging noise interfere with the radio's receiver?
Data and USB
- Is the data link pass-through, monitored, or used? USB 2.0, USB-C, or a military protocol on the connector? Who owns the protocol definition?
- USB power: legacy USB 2.0 supplies 5 V at up to 500 mA; USB-C advertises 1.5 A or 3 A at 5 V by its resistors; USB Power Delivery negotiates higher voltages and power. Which does each port actually support, and is it certified or merely compatible? (published: usb.org specifications)
- SMBus: does the device read battery data (state of charge, temperature, faults), and what does it do with it?
Reliability and safety
- Parts derated per the company's or customer's derating rules (voltage, current, power, temperature)?
- Thermal analysis done for the worst case (hot day, full load, sealed enclosure)?
- Fault cases reviewed: a single failed part shouldn't overcharge a battery or overheat on the soldier. (A design FMEA is the tool: https://ancreationoffice.com/design-risk.txt)
- Battery safety and transport: does the device contain cells (then UN 38.3 for shipping, IEC 62133-2 or UL 2054 as applicable), or only connect to them?
- Counterfeit-part controls on electronic parts (sop-mil-program.txt).

SOLDIER POWER AND RADIO INTERFACES (published; confirm against the program's interface documents)
- Conformal Wearable Battery (CWB): a flexible lithium-ion battery worn in the vest (Inventus Power, Woodridge, Illinois, and others). The Army's battery site lists output of about 10 to 20 V depending on version and charge, a Nett Warrior connector (NATO STANAG 4695) and bottom contacts; version 3.6.2 and later add a USB-A port. Data over SMBus. Source: https://battery.army.mil/warfighter-hub/conformal-wearable-battery/
- Nett Warrior connector: the Glenair Series 807 "Mighty Mouse" Nett Warrior connector family, 6-pin and a newer 7-pin version with USB-C power; adopted as STANAG 4695. Hubs such as Glenair STAR-PAN distribute power and data. Source: https://cdn.glenair.com/catalogs/mighty-mouse-807-nw-connector.pdf
- BB-2590: the common rechargeable lithium-ion military battery; also a power source on some systems.
- Handheld radios such as the L3Harris AN/PRC-152A and AN/PRC-163 use their own lithium-ion batteries; L3Harris and others sell power adapters that run the radio from external DC and charge its battery (for example, the L3Harris RF-7912 power adapter). Those products are a useful benchmark for behaviors (switch-over, charge, priority). Source: https://www.l3harris.com/sites/default/files/2020-12/cs-tcom-rf-7912-ps00x-power-adapter-spec-sheet.pdf
- Power scavengers: Iris Technology's Vulture Power Scavenger sits in-line between the soldier's system battery (a CWB or 2590) and the rest of the kit. It takes any DC source from 5 to 46 V (solar included), uses it to offset the system battery's discharge, and charges the battery with any excess as an SMBus Level 3 charger; built for Nett Warrior, -40 to +55 C operating, MOLLE-mounted. Iris documents disagree on maximum output (5 A or 10 A at 16.8 V); check the current datasheet. Iris's Merlin HH+ does the same job at the AN/PRC-163 radio: runs it from external power and charges its battery. Both are close benchmarks for this kind of device: their priority rules, charge behavior, connectors and size. Sources: https://www.iristechnology.com/tactical/man-portable/vulture-power-scavenger ; https://www.militaryaerospace.com/communications/product/55378085/iris-technology-introduces-rugged-power-management-adapter-for-an-prc-163-radio
- Interface control documents for these come through the program or the prime contractor and may be controlled; record that they exist and where they are, not their content.
